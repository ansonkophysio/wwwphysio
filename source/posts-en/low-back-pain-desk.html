After a full day at your desk, does your back feel stiff and sore when you stand up? This article explains low back pain common among office workers and introduces three simple exercises.

In shortMost low back pain has no single serious cause; clinical guidelines call it "non-specific low back pain", and most of it improves gradually. Guidelines recommend staying as active as possible and avoiding prolonged bed rest, and most people do not need an immediate X-ray or MRI. If you lose control of your bladder or bowels, have numbness around the groin, or develop weakness in both legs, seek medical help immediately.

What causes low back pain

Most low back pain cannot be traced to a single, clear structural problem. Clinical guidelines call this "non-specific low back pain".

Holding one sitting position for a long time can leave the back feeling stiff and tired.

With back pain, staying active is usually better than bed rest.

Research comparing advice to rest in bed with advice to stay active found that people with acute low back pain who stayed active had slightly better pain and function.

Guidelines also state that most back pain does not need an immediate X-ray or MRI; a doctor will arrange imaging if something serious is suspected.

Three simple exercises

Choose exercises that feel comfortable and that you can keep up, without making the pain clearly worse. The numbers below are a starting point only.

Points to note

  • Move slowly and stay within a comfortable range.
  • If the pain clearly worsens during an exercise, stop.
  • Keep up your daily activities as far as possible, building up gradually.

When to seek help immediately

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or difficulty passing urine
  • Numbness around the groin or inner thighs
  • Weakness in both legs, or numbness that keeps getting worse
  • Fever, or back pain after a serious injury such as a fall from height
  • Unexplained weight loss

If the pain has not improved after four weeks, or comes with leg pain or numbness, please also see a doctor or physiotherapist.

Common questions

Should I rest in bed with back pain?

Prolonged bed rest is generally not recommended. Research found that people with acute low back pain who stayed active had slightly better pain and function. Adjust your activity level to your pain.

Do I need an X-ray or MRI for back pain?

In most cases, not immediately. Guidelines recommend imaging only when something serious is suspected, and a doctor decides based on your situation.

Which exercise is best for back pain?

Guidelines recommend exercise therapy for persistent back pain. The right exercise varies from person to person, and a physiotherapist can advise on what suits you.
