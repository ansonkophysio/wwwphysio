Landed on someone's foot during a game, or missed a step on a hike, and your ankle rolled and swelled? This article explains how to manage an ankle sprain, how long recovery takes, and when you can return to sport.

In shortThe most common ankle sprain injures the ligaments on the outside of the ankle. If you cannot walk four steps on your own after the injury, or certain bones around the ankle or foot are clearly tender to touch, seek medical advice first to check for a fracture. Research supports early movement within what you can tolerate, and exercise to help the ankle regain stability. Pain usually eases a lot in the first two weeks, but some people still have pain or a feeling of instability a year later; balance training and an ankle brace help reduce the risk of spraining it again.

First: rule out a fracture

After a sprain, the first priority is to rule out a fracture. Doctors often use the Ottawa ankle rules to decide whether an X-ray is needed. Research shows these rules reliably rule out fractures of the ankle and mid-foot and reduce unnecessary X-rays.

Seek medical advice first if

  • You cannot walk four steps on your own, both immediately after the injury and when you are seen
  • The back edge or tip of the bone on the inside or outside of the ankle is clearly tender
  • The middle of the outer edge of the foot (base of the fifth metatarsal) or the inner side of the mid-foot (navicular) is clearly tender
  • Your foot is numb, changes colour or feels cold

How are ankle sprains graded?

Clinicians usually grade an ankle sprain into three levels by how badly the ligaments are injured:

GradeLigamentsWhat it is often like
Grade 1 (mild)Stretched, without a clear tearMild pain and swelling; walking is usually still possible
Grade 2 (moderate)Partly tornMore obvious swelling and bruising; walking is painful
Grade 3 (severe)Completely tornMarked swelling and bruising; hard to bear weight; the ankle may feel loose

Grading needs an examination by a clinician. Pain and swelling are greatest at first, and guidelines note that re-examining the ankle four to five days after the injury judges the ligament injury more reliably.

The first few days

Research supports early movement: start walking and moving the ankle as soon as possible, within what the pain allows. A short period of immobilisation may ease pain and swelling, but guidelines say the most helpful approach is to support the ankle with a brace or tape while doing exercise.

Anti-inflammatory painkillers can reduce pain and swelling, but they have side effects and may interfere with the body's natural healing. If you need to take them, ask a doctor or pharmacist first.

After a sprain, the focus is on retraining the ankle's stability and balance.

Guidelines recommend supervised exercise as the main treatment, as it helps the ankle regain stability better than passive treatments alone.

Ice or heat?

This is often the first question after a sprain. Reviews of the research show limited evidence for ice: there is some evidence that ice combined with exercise works better, but studies could not establish the best way to apply ice or for how long.

Early movement and exercise have stronger research support. If ice makes you more comfortable, it can be an add-on; wrap the ice pack in a towel so ice does not touch the skin directly.

How long does an ankle sprain take to heal?

Recovery time differs between people, depending on how bad the injury is and on rehabilitation. A review of 31 studies found that:

  • pain usually eases a lot within the first two weeks;
  • a year later, 5% to 33% of people still had pain;
  • within three years, 36% to 85% reported full recovery;
  • 3% to 34% sprained the ankle again.

In other words, no pain does not mean full recovery. Even once the pain has gone, it is worth finishing balance and strength training.

Points to note

  • Stay within what the pain allows, and move slowly.
  • When balancing on one leg, keep something to hold on to nearby.
  • Before returning to ball sports or hiking, make sure you can balance on one leg, rise onto your toes and jog without clear pain.

When can I return to sport?

Returning to sport should not depend only on how many days have passed. An international expert consensus recommends considering five areas before deciding:

  • Pain: during activity and over the past 24 hours
  • The ankle itself: range of motion, and muscle strength, endurance and power
  • Confidence: whether you trust the ankle, feel it is stable and feel ready
  • Balance: sense of joint position and dynamic balance
  • Performance: hopping, jumping and changing direction, sport-specific drills, and completing a full training session

Why do I keep spraining my ankle?

After an ankle sprain, some people develop chronic ankle instability: the ankle often feels loose and sprains again easily. A study that followed people for a year after a first sprain found that those who could not complete landing and jumping tasks two weeks after the injury, and those with poorer dynamic balance at six months, were more likely to have chronic ankle instability at one year.

Reviews of the research show strong evidence for bracing, and moderate evidence for neuromuscular training such as balance training, in preventing repeat sprains.

Common questions

Can I walk on a sprained ankle?

If you can bear weight, research supports early movement within what the pain allows. If you cannot walk four steps on your own, seek medical advice first.

How long does the swelling take to go down?

It differs from person to person. Research found that pain usually eases a lot within the first two weeks. If swelling persists or gets worse, or any of the warning signs in this article appear, seek medical advice early.

Do I need an ankle brace?

Reviews show strong evidence that a brace helps prevent repeat sprains, and guidelines recommend a brace or tape with exercise after the injury. Whether you need one, and for how long, is worth discussing with a physiotherapist.

Will I need surgery for an ankle sprain?

Most ankle sprains do not need surgery. Guidelines say surgery should be kept for people who have not improved after a full course of exercise-based treatment.
