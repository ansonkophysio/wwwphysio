Does a herniated disc always need surgery? Three facts that are often overlooked

Back pain after long hours at a desk, then an MRI report that says "disc herniation". Does that mean you need surgery? This article explains three facts that are often overlooked.

In shortNot necessarily. Many people without back pain have a disc herniation on MRI. Even when a herniation irritates a nerve and causes leg pain, conservative treatment can usually be tried first if there are no warning signs, and some herniated discs shrink over time. Whether surgery is needed depends mainly on your symptoms and nerve function, not on how large the herniation looks on the scan. If you suddenly lose control of your bladder or bowels, have numbness around the groin or buttocks, or your leg is getting weaker, go to the emergency department immediately.

What is a herniated disc?

Each disc sits between two vertebrae. It has a tough outer ring and a softer centre. When the outer ring tears, part of the centre can push outwards: this is a disc herniation.

If the material irritates or presses on a nearby nerve, it can cause pain, numbness or weakness running from the buttock down the leg, often called "sciatica", as well as back pain.

Fact 1: a herniation on MRI does not mean it explains all the pain

A systematic review of 33 studies covering more than 3,000 people without back pain found disc herniation (protrusion) on imaging in about 29% of 20-year-olds, rising to about 43% of 80-year-olds.

In other words, many changes seen on scans are part of normal ageing. An MRI report needs to be read alongside your symptoms and examination findings; the scan alone cannot show where the pain comes from.

Whether you need surgery depends on your symptoms, not on how big the herniation is.

Fact 2: even with leg pain, many people can try conservative treatment first

A study published in the New England Journal of Medicine divided 283 people who had had sciatica for 6 to 12 weeks into two groups: one had early surgery, and the other had conservative treatment first, with surgery only if needed.

In the conservative group, about six in ten did not need surgery within a year. Leg pain eased faster in the early-surgery group, but after one year the two groups had recovered to a similar degree.

A 2023 analysis of 24 trials found something similar: surgery relieved leg pain more in the short term, but the difference narrowed over time and was small at around one year, with no clear difference in the risk of adverse events. The certainty of this evidence is low, however. Surgery remains an option, particularly for people who want faster relief and have weighed the risks with their doctor.

Fact 3: a herniated disc can shrink on its own

The body can gradually reabsorb material squeezed out of a disc. A 2023 systematic review found that, in people who did not have surgery, about half (52.5%) of herniations of the "protrusion" type had shrunk on follow-up scans taken an average of about 11.5 months later.

Even so, whether a herniation shrinks on a scan is not the only guide to treatment. The decision about surgery still rests mainly on symptoms and nerve function.

What does conservative treatment involve?

Conservative treatment can include exercise, physiotherapy, and medicines as advised by your doctor. During this time, stay as active as you can and avoid long periods of bed rest.

The right exercises vary from person to person and should be based on an assessment. If there is no improvement after several months, or the pain is seriously affecting your life, discuss with your doctor whether surgery is needed.

Points to note during conservative treatment

  • Keep up your daily activities as far as possible, adjusting to your pain.
  • Avoid staying in one position for long; get up and walk regularly.
  • If the area of pain or numbness keeps spreading, seek medical advice promptly.

When to seek help immediately

Go to the emergency department immediately if you have:

  • sudden loss of bladder or bowel control, or difficulty passing urine
  • numbness around the groin or buttocks
  • weakness in the leg that is getting worse

If symptoms persist, the pain seriously disturbs your sleep or daily life, or you have a fever or unexplained weight loss, please also see a doctor or physiotherapist.

Common questions

Will a herniated disc get better on its own?

Symptoms improve over time for many people, and some herniations shrink, but progress varies from person to person. If symptoms get worse or warning signs appear, seek medical advice promptly.

When should surgery be considered?

Warning signs such as loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or worsening leg weakness need immediate medical attention. Beyond that, if leg pain is still severe and affecting your life after a period of conservative treatment, discuss the benefits and risks of surgery with an orthopaedic surgeon or neurosurgeon.

Do I need another MRI to check whether it has shrunk?

Progress is usually judged by changes in symptoms and function. Whether repeat imaging is needed is a decision for your doctor.

Can I exercise with a herniated disc?

Staying active is generally recommended, and exercise is part of conservative treatment. The right type and amount vary from person to person, so ask a doctor or physiotherapist.
